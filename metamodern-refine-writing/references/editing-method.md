# Editing Method

## Outcome

A refinement improves the reader's ability to understand, decide, respond, or act. It does not replace the author with a house voice.

Work from the draft's purpose and evidence. Diagnose before editing. Use the smallest edit that solves a real problem.

## Choose the posture

### Voice-bearing writing

Use this posture for email, personal writing, brand language, strategy, narrative, social writing, proposals, and client communication.

Preserve:

- first-person stance and characteristic vocabulary;
- contractions, fragments, repetition, slang, profanity, metaphor, and uneven rhythm when they carry voice;
- humor, skepticism, anger, warmth, hesitation, and self-interruption;
- deliberate ambiguity or unresolved thought;
- the level of polish that fits the author and surface.

Do not turn voice-bearing writing into corporate copy, technical prose, or uniformly polished paragraphs.

### Operational and technical writing

Use this posture for documentation, instructions, status, decision records, errors, reports, and implementation notes.

Prefer:

- one stable name for one concept;
- named actors when responsibility matters;
- direct verbs instead of abstract action nouns;
- conditions before the actions they control;
- one instruction per step;
- one topic per paragraph;
- specific mechanisms, limits, and outcomes when the source supplies them.

Keep necessary technical terms, safety language, qualifiers, and formal requirements. Do not force short sentences when a longer sentence preserves an exact condition or relationship.

## Build the preservation ledger

Before editing, record these internally:

### Meaning and evidence

- claims, facts, examples, numbers, dates, names, and quotations;
- conditions, exceptions, scope boundaries, and dependencies;
- certainty and uncertainty: known, observed, likely, possible, unverified, disputed;
- degree and stance: unique, meaningful, strong, weak, better, worse, preferred, opposed;
- commitments, decisions, requests, deadlines, approvals, and status;
- causal claims and the evidence that supports them.

### Voice

- vocabulary and recurring phrases;
- sentence length, cadence, fragments, and paragraph rhythm;
- bluntness, warmth, humor, profanity, skepticism, and restraint;
- personal setup, digressions, images, and unusual comparisons;
- the draft's intended level of polish.

### Reader utility

- the point the reader needs;
- the action, answer, decision, or response the draft seeks;
- the order in which the reader needs the information;
- any part that the reader must find quickly.

## Diagnose actual problems

### Empty setup

Cut throat-clearing, assistant scaffolding, generic scene-setting, and explanations of what the draft is about. Keep personal setup when it supplies context, tension, or character.

### Inflated language

Translate grand claims, promotional adjectives, vague abstractions, and significance language into plainer terms without removing the author's position or changing its degree. If the source lacks support, preserve the claim and flag the evidence gap outside the draft. Delete a claim only when it is genuinely semantically empty or repeats meaning carried elsewhere.

### Buried point or ask

Move the answer, decision, request, or next action earlier when the current order makes the reader work to find it. Do not reorder a narrative only because a direct structure looks tidier.

### Repetition without development

Remove repeated conclusions, duplicated context, and transitions that restate the prior sentence. Keep repetition used for voice, emphasis, pacing, or argument development.

### Weak actors and verbs

Use an active construction when passive or nominalized language hides who acted or what happened. Keep passive voice when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, intentionally withheld, or less important than the result.

### Empty hedge vs meaningful uncertainty

An empty hedge avoids taking a position without changing the claim. Meaningful uncertainty limits the claim.

- Empty: "It could perhaps be argued that the rollout was somewhat delayed."
- Meaningful: "The rollout may slip if the vendor misses the August 30 shipment."

Remove the first kind. Preserve the second kind and its condition.

### Synthetic shape

Watch for every paragraph landing the same way, forced symmetry, generic conclusions, excessive headings, ornamental bullets, and repeated sentence openings. Repair the local problem without making the whole draft follow another template.

### Reader friction

Untangle vague pronouns, missing antecedents, noun clusters, unexplained jargon, and sentences that combine unrelated claims. Preserve technical density when the reader needs it.

## Make proportional edits

Use this order:

1. Delete text that carries no meaning or voice.
2. Clarify vague references and hidden actors.
3. Replace inflated phrasing with the supported fact.
4. Reorder only when the reader cannot find the point, ask, or decision.
5. Split or combine sentences only when the change improves comprehension or cadence.
6. Stop when every remaining edit earns its place.

Do not replace one cliche with another. Do not smooth every edge. Do not add specificity the author did not supply.

## Final comparison

Compare the source and revision for:

- every preservation-ledger item;
- any change in certainty, stance, responsibility, or causal meaning;
- any voice signal removed without a reader benefit;
- any new claim, promise, recommendation, or implied approval;
- any sentence changed only because a different version sounded more polished.

If the draft already meets the outcome, return it unchanged and say that no edit was necessary.
