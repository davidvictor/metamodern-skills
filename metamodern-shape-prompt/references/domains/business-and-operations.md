# Business and operations

Use this reference when a rough thought concerns commercial choices, organizational work, or agreements. For Clarify, make the existing thought clearer and keep its scope. For Expand, use the distinctions and questions to develop three meaningfully different prompt directions. Neither mode authorizes carrying out the resulting work.

For deeper audience, positioning, channel, growth, and measurement distinctions, use [Marketing](marketing.md). For persuasive message choices, use [Copywriting](copywriting.md). This reference retains the business, money, process, and authority distinctions that may constrain either task.

## Market meaning and customer behavior

Positioning concerns who an offer is for, what it means relative to alternatives, and why someone would choose it. Acquisition concerns gaining customers. Conversion concerns a specified transition, such as inquiry to purchase. Retention concerns continuing a relationship or use over time. Awareness, purchase, and continued use differ.

"People don't get why we're different" may concern positioning or communicating a difference. It does not establish that the offer lacks differentiation. "We need more customers" names an outcome without identifying whether reach, relevance, conversion, or another constraint is responsible. "They buy once and disappear" points toward retention questions, but does not explain the cause.

Useful exploration questions:

- Which audience and alternative does the speaker have in mind?
- Where does the desired behavior stop happening?
- Is the request to clarify value, investigate a constraint, or choose an action?

## Price, cost, and money timing

Price is what a customer is charged. Cost concerns resources consumed or obligations incurred. Revenue concerns amounts earned from sales; profit concerns revenue relative to costs under the accounting basis being used. Cash concerns money available and its movement over time. An invoice amount, an agreed fee, and cash received should remain separate facts.

"We're busy but there's no money" could concern profitability, payment timing, spending, or a mix. Preserve the uncertainty. "We're too cheap" might mean the price does not reflect perceived value, the work consumes too many resources, or the speaker wants a different market position. Do not turn it automatically into "raise prices."

Useful exploration questions:

- Is the concern the amount charged, resources required, or timing of payment?
- Is the speaker describing a verified figure, an estimate, or a feeling?
- What comparison gives "too expensive" or "not enough" its meaning?

Use these distinctions to clarify a request, not to supply financial advice, rates, forecasts, or commercial terms.

## Organization, process, and authority

Process concerns how work moves. Capacity concerns available ability to perform it. Ownership identifies who is responsible for a result. Decision rights identify who can choose or approve. Accountability concerns who must explain the result. Coordination concerns dependencies among participants.

"Everything comes back to me" could describe concentrated approval authority, unclear ownership, missing information, or limited capability elsewhere. "We need a better system" does not yet specify software or a new process. "Nobody owns this" may mean nobody performs the task, nobody decides, or nobody follows through.

Explore where work waits, who has authority, what information is missing, and what outcome the speaker wants to delegate. Do not invent reporting lines or assume that more procedure is the answer.

## Negotiation and agreements

A position is a stated demand or proposal. An interest is the concern it serves. A constraint limits acceptable options. Agreement requires actual assent; silence, discussion, and a draft do not establish it.

"I don't want to give this away" may express a boundary about price, scope, credit, or control. Keep the boundary visible without inventing the other party's motives. Explore what is negotiable, what must remain protected, and what would count as agreement. Do not draft binding terms, promise concessions, contact another party, or treat clarification as permission to negotiate.
