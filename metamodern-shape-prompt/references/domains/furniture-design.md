# Furniture Design Language

Use this dictionary when the thought concerns furniture: seating, tables, desks, beds, cabinets, shelving, storage, or built-in pieces. It supplies language for form, use, materials, construction, and development. Clarify names what the user already means; Expand uses relevant distinctions to open possible directions. Neither performs the design or turns an aesthetic preference into a fabrication specification.

## Work areas and boundaries

| Work area | Use when the intent concerns |
| --- | --- |
| Furniture design | A piece's form, proportions, details, function, comfort, or relationship to its setting |
| Industrial design / product development | Developing a physical product through materials, construction, prototypes, manufacturing, and repeatability |
| Ergonomics | How the piece accommodates bodies, posture, reach, movement, and intended use |
| Materials and finishes | Substrate, wood grain, upholstery, texture, color, sheen, touch, or surface treatment |
| Joinery and construction | Connections, supports, assembly, hardware, and how the piece holds together |
| Cabinetry / millwork | Cabinet cases, doors, drawers, fitted storage, and architectural woodwork |
| Upholstery design and development | The relationship between covering, padding, support, seams, and upholstered form |
| Interior design / space planning | Selecting and arranging pieces in a room, circulation, and relationships among furniture, architecture, and use |
| Furniture production | Fabrication processes, repeatability, sourcing, assembly, packing, and production constraints |

Furniture design concerns the piece; interior design concerns its setting and arrangement. A chair is not automatically an ergonomics assignment, and a cabinet is not automatically a manufacturing assignment. Use the decision the user names. In this domain, product design refers to physical objects rather than automatically to screens or UX.

## Form, proportion, and detail

| Term | Meaning and useful distinction |
| --- | --- |
| Silhouette / profile | The visible outline; profile often refers to a particular view or cross-sectional shape |
| Proportion / scale | Relationships among a piece's parts / its size relative to people, surroundings, or other objects |
| Visual mass | How heavy, dense, or substantial a form appears; distinct from physical weight |
| Negative space | Open areas between or around parts, such as beneath a seat or between supports |
| Stance / footprint | How the piece appears to sit on its supports / the floor area it occupies |
| Taper / splay | A gradual change in a member's thickness / an outward angle of legs or supports |
| Edge profile | The shape of an edge; a chamfer is a bevel, while a rounded edge has a curved transition |
| Overhang / apron / stretcher | A surface extending beyond its support / a member beneath a tabletop connecting supports / a member connecting legs or supports, often lower down |
| Flush / inset / overlay | Surfaces aligned in the same plane / a door or drawer front fitted within an opening / a front covering part of the surrounding case or frame |
| Reveal | A visible gap or setback between adjacent elements; even-looking reveals do not establish a numerical tolerance |
| Case / carcass | The main box or body of a cabinet or storage piece, distinct from its doors, drawers, and fittings |

Use words such as sculptural, restrained, monolithic, airy, tactile, or domestic as descriptions of the desired effect when supported. Do not silently replace “warm” or “modern” with a historical style, designer attribution, wood species, or material palette.

## Materials, construction, and comfort

| Term or distinction | What to preserve |
| --- | --- |
| Solid wood / veneer / substrate | Solid wood is wood throughout the relevant part; veneer is a thin layer of wood over a supporting material. Visible wood grain alone does not establish construction. |
| Grain direction / figure | The orientation of wood fibers / the visible pattern in the wood. Keep appearance separate from structural conclusions. |
| Finish / sheen / texture | Surface treatment / degree of gloss / surface feel or visible surface character. “Matte” describes sheen, not a specific coating. |
| Joinery / hardware | How parts connect / fittings such as hinges, slides, and fasteners. Exposed joinery is also a visual choice; appearance alone does not prove strength. |
| Mortise-and-tenon / dowel joint | A projecting tenon fitted into a mortise / parts connected with dowels. These name methods only when stated or being explored as possibilities. |
| Knock-down / flat-pack | Designed to be assembled and disassembled / packaged in a compact, relatively flat form. One does not guarantee the other. |
| Modularity | Parts or units can be combined or reconfigured within a system; this does not establish tool-free assembly or universal compatibility. |
| Upholstery / padding / support | The covering and upholstered assembly / cushioning layers / the underlying structure or suspension. Surface softness does not establish overall comfort. |
| Seat height / depth / back angle | Distinct relationships to posture and use. Desired comfort does not supply dimensions or one universally suitable posture. |
| Clearance / reach | Space for bodies, movement, and moving parts / access to a surface, control, or storage area. Preserve whose use and which setting are known. |
| Stability / stiffness / strength | Resistance to tipping / resistance to deformation / ability to withstand load without failure. A substantial appearance proves none of these. |
| Durability / repairability | How well a piece withstands use over time / how readily parts or surfaces can be repaired. Neither follows automatically from a material label. |
| Mock-up / prototype / production specification | A study of selected qualities / a version used to explore or test the design / defined requirements for making it. A concept image is not fabrication evidence. |

## Translate the desired effect

| Rough expression | Possible precise language when context supports it |
| --- | --- |
| “It looks heavy, but I don't mean the weight.” | Reduce visual mass through the relationships among solid form, supports, and negative space; keep the method open |
| “The legs should get thinner toward the floor.” | Legs that taper toward the floor; this does not imply outward splay |
| “The doors should sit level with the front.” | Flush front surfaces; do not assume a particular hinge or opening mechanism |
| “The spaces around the drawers look uneven.” | Uneven reveals around drawer fronts; preserve the observation without inventing a tolerance or cause |
| “Soft looking, but not a sofa you sink into.” | A soft upholstered appearance with a more supported sitting experience; do not prescribe foam density |
| “It should feel solid without looking bulky.” | A sense of solidity with reduced visual bulk; distinguish perceived solidity from verified stability or strength |
| “It should grow with the room.” | Possibly modular or reconfigurable furniture, or a piece that suits different settings; keep the intended meaning open |
| “I want the wood to feel natural, not shiny.” | A natural tactile character and low sheen; species, coating, and maintenance remain undecided |

These are candidate meanings, not automatic substitutions or solutions. Preserve uncertainty about whether an effect comes from form, materials, construction, or setting.

## Questions for expansion

Choose angles specific to the seed. A visually heavy chair might support exploring silhouette and negative space, the relationship between visual lightness and perceived solidity, or how its scale reads beside a dining table. Those are different inquiries, not three compulsory phases.

A storage thought might support exploring front alignment and reveals, access and opening behavior, or how the cabinet relates to the room. Carry explicit exclusions and unresolved choices into every standalone prompt. Introduce new approaches as possible directions, not decisions the user has made.

Keep dimensions, loads, wood species, finishes, hardware, joinery choices, foam specifications, costs, production quantities, and compliance claims open unless supplied. Words such as sustainable, ergonomic, or contract-grade express aims or claims to examine; they do not establish certification, testing, or suitability.
