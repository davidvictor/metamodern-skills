# Research and human context

Use this reference for inquiry, interpretation, creative thinking, arguments, learning, and relationships. For Clarify, preserve the speaker's claim, uncertainty, and emotional meaning while clarifying the language. For Expand, develop distinct questions or approaches that deepen the thought. Neither mode begins research, produces the downstream artifact, or acts on another person.

## Observation, interpretation, and cause

An observation describes what was noticed or recorded. An interpretation proposes what it means. A hypothesis offers an explanation that could be examined. Association describes things varying together. A causal claim says one thing changes another.

"People stopped replying after we changed the message" reports a sequence if the speaker observed it. "The message drove them away" adds a causal interpretation. "This proves my point" may express conviction; preserve that stance while making the requested examination clear, rather than upgrading the evidence yourself.

Useful exploration questions:

- What was directly observed, and what is inferred?
- Which alternative explanation would matter to the question?
- What finding would change the speaker's view?

Distinguish exploration from confirmation. Preserve an explicit intent to defend a position without presenting it as fact.

## Measurement and evidence

A concept is the thing of interest, such as trust or usefulness. A measure is how some aspect is recorded. A proxy stands in for something not measured directly. A sample is the portion examined.

"I want to know if this works" needs an intended outcome and context, not an automatic dashboard. "Everyone hated it" may be a vivid account of a few reactions, a broad claim, or deliberate exaggeration. Keep the voice while avoiding invented counts or representativeness. "The numbers look better" leaves open which numbers, compared with what, and whether the change matters.

Explore what success would mean, whose experience matters, what evidence is available, and what the measure might miss. Do not invent findings, certainty, or sample sizes. Expand may propose a way to investigate, but must not present it as a method the user already selected.

## Creative work, argument, and learning

A theme is a recurring concern or meaning. A premise establishes a starting situation or proposition. A claim states what an argument asks the reader to accept. Reasons support it; objections challenge it. An example illustrates a point without necessarily establishing its general truth. Learning may seek recall, understanding, application, or judgment, which need different requests.

"There's something here, but I can't say it" may call for concept development before polished prose. "Make this argument hit harder" could mean clearer reasoning, stronger evidence, or more forceful rhetoric. "I understand it until I have to use it" suggests exploring the gap between explanation and application.

Ask what should remain unresolved, what the audience should grasp or feel, and whether the speaker wants to discover an idea or express one already held. Do not impose a thesis, genre, teaching sequence, or creative interpretation the thought does not support.

## Interpersonal meaning

Separate an event, the speaker's interpretation, its felt impact, and the response they want. A request asks another person to act. A boundary states the speaker's own limits. An intention is not the same as an effect.

"They don't respect my time" may express repeated cancellations and feeling disregarded. Preserve the feeling without claiming access to the other person's mind. "I want to be honest without making this a fight" names both an expressive aim and a relational concern.

Explore what happened, what matters to the speaker, and what they want understood or changed. Avoid psychological diagnosis, invented motives, or certainty about another person's character. Do not turn an account of hurt into a demand for reconciliation, confrontation, or forgiveness, and do not send any message.
