# Automotive Design Language

Use this dictionary when a thought concerns a vehicle's exterior or interior form, spatial experience, material expression, or driver-facing interaction. It supplies language for shaping a prompt: Clarify names the supported intent; Expand opens distinct directions; Combine keeps design intent and constraints traceable; Prepare frames evidence without turning a visual preference into an engineering claim.

## Work areas and boundaries

| Work area | Use when the intent concerns |
| --- | --- |
| Automotive design | Exterior and interior form, proportion, surfaces, detail, atmosphere, or perceived character |
| Vehicle packaging | The spatial arrangement of occupants, components, luggage, visibility, and usable volume; it is not shipping or logistics |
| CMF | Colour, material, and finish: visual, tactile, and wear-facing material expression |
| Automotive HMI | Driver and passenger controls, displays, information, and feedback in a driving context |
| Automotive engineering | Structure, propulsion, energy, dynamics, thermal systems, software, manufacturing, or verification |
| Brand design | A marque's distinctive cues and recognition across vehicles and communications |

Automotive design concerns the intended experience and visible expression. It does not establish dimensions, aerodynamic performance, handling, durability, safety compliance, or a feasible production method. Use [Automotive Engineering](automotive-engineering.md) when the question is about those consequences, and [UI Design](ui-design.md) when the prompt principally concerns an interface rather than the vehicle context around it.

## Form, space, and material expression

| Term | Meaning and useful distinction |
| --- | --- |
| Proportion | Relationships among major volumes and features, such as wheel, cabin, body, and overhang relationships; it is not a set of dimensions |
| Stance | How a vehicle appears to sit on its wheels and meet the ground; perceived plantedness is not measured stability |
| Silhouette | The vehicle's overall outline from a stated view; it differs from a particular surface treatment |
| Greenhouse | The glazed upper body enclosing windows and pillars; it is not the whole cabin |
| Surface | A body panel's continuous form and transitions; a crisp line, radius, reflection, or highlight can change how it reads |
| Character line | A deliberate visual line or break that organizes a surface; it does not prove a panel construction method |
| Visual mass | How substantial a volume appears, distinct from vehicle mass or structural strength |
| Exterior / interior | The outward body and its relationship to road context / the occupant environment, controls, visibility, storage, and perceived space |
| Packaging | Spatial relationships that make a proposed form usable; a roomy appearance does not prove occupancy or cargo volume |
| CMF | Colour, material, and finish considered together; a material name alone does not define its finish or tactile character |
| Perceived quality | The impression of care, coherence, precision, and material credibility; it is not a durability, fit, or reliability result |
| Class-A surface | A visible styling surface developed for controlled form, transitions, and reflection quality; exact surface-quality criteria depend on the project, and this is not synonymous with perceived quality |

## Automotive HMI and attention

| Term or distinction | What to preserve |
| --- | --- |
| Driver attention | The limited attention available while driving; visual novelty does not demonstrate acceptable distraction or safety |
| Primary / secondary controls | Controls that directly govern vehicle motion / ancillary or supporting functions; frequency, urgency, and task priority are separate dimensions |
| Glanceable information | Information intended to be understood quickly; it still needs a stated task, condition, and evidence |
| Feedback | What the vehicle communicates after an action; separate visible, audible, and haptic feedback when relevant |
| Physical / digital control | A tangible input / a screen-based or software-mediated input. One does not automatically make the interaction better |
| Driver / passenger experience | The operating person / another occupant. Preserve whose task, reach, attention, and permission matter |

For HMI prompts, retain operating context, task urgency, and who uses the control. Interface guidelines can inform an expansion, but do not turn a concept into a claim of driver-distraction performance or compliance.

## Translate the desired effect

| Rough expression | Possible precise language when context supports it |
| --- | --- |
| “Make it feel planted.” | A more grounded visual stance through the relationship among body volume, wheels, and lower form; keep dynamic performance separate |
| “The cabin should feel open.” | An interior sense of openness through sightlines, glazing, surface continuity, or spatial organization; keep actual headroom and volume open |
| “More premium.” | A more coherent and credible expression of material, fit, detail, and interaction; identify which impression matters before specifying a material |
| “Sportier, but not aggressive.” | A more purposeful proportion, stance, or surface tension while avoiding hostile visual cues; keep aero and handling claims open |
| “Less screen-heavy.” | A different balance of digital and physical controls for stated driver or passenger tasks; do not assume a control architecture |
| “It should look aerodynamic.” | A visually streamlined silhouette or surface treatment; distinguish this from measured aerodynamic performance |

These are candidate meanings, not replacements to apply automatically. The same desired effect may arise from proportion, surface, materials, HMI, or context.

## Questions for expansion

Choose angles that open genuinely different design questions. An exterior concept might explore how silhouette changes its brand recognition, how packaging pressure shapes its profile, or how surfaces change its visual mass. An interior thought might explore occupant orientation and visibility, CMF atmosphere, or the attention demands of a specific control task.

When design and engineering are both in scope, Combine can retain a shared chain: design intent -> relevant constraints -> tradeoffs -> owner -> evidence. Keep each link optional unless the user has established it, and preserve supplied facts separately from design hypotheses.

## Evidence boundaries and primary sources

Concept sketches, renderings, and language about a vehicle establish intended expression, not measured capability. Preserve unknown dimensions, drag, mass, performance, tuning, manufacturing readiness, testing, and safety or regulatory status. The sources below describe design-process and human-factors context; they do not establish facts about a particular vehicle.
